# 太平惠民和劑局方 指南總論 卷上 004 論用薬法 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

On the method of using medicines.

Of the ways of rescuing the age, none is greater than medicine; of the merits of removing illness, none comes before drugs. People live amid the five phases and four qi, and illness arises from summer heat, damp, wind and cold. Drugs are divided into three grades and seven relations, with natures warm, balanced, cold or hot. In using them there must be no error. If one wishes to establish a formula, it must rest on a basis; if the treatment accords with principle, no illness will fail to heal. If one is ignorant of new and old, does not distinguish genuine from false, uses drugs one-sidedly and sparsely, gives them other names, seeks the strange, does not know their natures, speaks wildly of their effects and decides by one's own inclinations, this is extremely rash. Hence the saying, "If a physician is not of the third generation, do not take his medicine"; these words are surely true—must one not think deeply? Further, one must not use what the locality lacks, what is costly and hard to buy, pearls and other treasures, or things rarely heard of: even the rich have nowhere to seek them, and the poor lack the means. Some seek drugs in distant lands, some cling to ancient formulas and cannot adapt, delaying in deliberation; by then the illness is deep—how can medicine treat it? Therefore the physician must abandon shortcomings and follow strengths, leave the complicated for the simple, rolling up and spreading out at will, expanding and contracting with the occasion, weighing what is fitting, adding and subtracting appropriately, examining the severity of the illness and using drugs with precision; then he may be called a superior practitioner.

Drugs have sovereign, minister, assistant and envoy, which diffuse, restrain and combine with one another. One sovereign, two ministers, three assistants and five envoys is suitable; one sovereign, three ministers and nine assistants and envoys is also possible. There is also the pairing of yin and yang (Zhang Yuxi and others note, citing the Shu edition's commentary: "All things of heaven and earth have yin and yang, and large and small each have their kinds of color. Investigating the principle, all have their patterns. Thus feathered creatures are all born of yang and belong to yin; scaled and shelled creatures are all born of yin and belong to yang. Therefore azurite follows wood, so it is green and governs the liver; cinnabar follows fire, so it is red and governs the heart; mica follows metal, so it is white and governs the lungs; orpiment follows earth, so it is yellow and governs the spleen; magnetite follows water, so it is black and governs the kidneys. The rest may be inferred from this, and conversely known."), mother and child, elder and younger brother (Zhang Yuxi and others note, citing the Shu edition's commentary: "Such as elm bark being the mother and magnolia bark the child."), root, stem, flower and fruit, herb, tree, bone and flesh. Further, there are drugs that act alone, that mutually reinforce, that mutually assist, that mutually fear, that are mutually averse, that mutually oppose and that mutually kill. These seven relations must be attended to when compounding. It is good to use those that mutually reinforce and assist; do not use those that are mutually averse or opposing. If a toxic drug needs restraining, those that mutually fear or kill may be used; otherwise they must not be combined. (Zhang Yuxi and others respectfully note, citing the Shu edition's commentary: "Of the 365 kinds, 71 act alone, 12 mutually reinforce, 90 mutually assist, 78 mutually fear, 60 are mutually averse, 18 mutually oppose and 36 mutually kill. These seven relations are to be observed in compounding.") There are also the five flavors—sour, salty, sweet, bitter and pungent—and the four qi—cold, hot, warm and cool—and toxic and non-toxic, shade-dried and sun-dried, the seasons for gathering and preparing, raw and cooked, place of origin, genuine and false, new and old; each has its method.

The months for gathering drugs all take *yin* as the first month of the year, as recorded after the Han Taichu calendar. Root drugs are mostly gathered in the second and eighth months, because at the beginning of spring the fluids are moist and just sprouting, not yet rushing up into branches and leaves, so their force is pure and rich; and in autumn the branches and leaves wither and the fluids return and flow downward. Testing this in practice now, spring is better early and autumn better late. Flowers, fruits, stems and leaves are each gathered when ripe. The years also run early or late, so one need not follow the text strictly.

Where the *Bencao* says "dry in the shade", it means drying within the yin of the six *jia*. According to the Dunjia method, the yin of the *jiazi* decade lies in *guiyou*, so the drugs are placed in the *you* direction. In fact this is not necessary; it simply means not exposing them to the sun but drying them in shady places; hence some are also said to be "sun-dried". (Now, examining the *Bencao*, drugs gathered and dried in the shade mostly spoil. Deer antler velvet, for example: the *Classic* says that dried in the shade it all rots and spoils, while drying it over a fire is now easier and better. Roots and shoots of herbs and trees all spoil in the shade; those gathered before the ninth month should be sun-dried, those gathered after the tenth month are better shade-dried.) If it is fortunately possible, use [this method] to make them even better.

## Notes

On the method of using medicines: the need for principled prescribing (warning against ignorance of new and old or genuine and false and against seeking the exotic; "if a physician is not of the third generation, do not take his medicine"); avoiding rare and costly drugs; adapting rather than clinging to ancient formulas; the structure of sovereign, minister, assistant and envoy (one sovereign, two ministers, three assistants, five envoys, etc.); yin-yang pairing (colors and zang organs); mother-child relations; the seven relations and their counts; five flavors and four qi; seasons for gathering (roots in the second and eighth months; early in spring, late in autumn); and the meaning of "drying in the shade".
- The small-print notes "Zhang Yuxi and others note, citing the Shu edition's commentary" quote the *Jiayou bencao*. Most of the text derives from the preface of the *Shennong bencao jing* and Tao Hongjing's commentary.
