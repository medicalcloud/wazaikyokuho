# 太平惠民和劑局方 指南總論 卷上 002 論合和法 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

On the method of compounding.

In compounding decoctions and medicines, strive for precision and concentration: distinguish new from old, identify the place of origin, prepare according to proper measure, weigh without error, and use them fittingly, and no illness will fail to heal. If genuine and false are of different kinds, cold and heat contradict each other, the sweetness and pungency of herbs and minerals are not understood, roasting and frying lose their natural properties, sifting is coarse and poor, and dosages are mistaken, then though it bears the name of treating illness, it will never have the effect of certain cure. Therefore the physician must pay earnest attention and take repeated care, and must not entrust others with compounding. [Otherwise] not only will the quantities be unequal, but the intent of the original formula will also be lost; after pounding and mixing, good and bad are hard to tell, many mouths taste it and many noses smell it, its essential qi is entirely spent, and when it is used to treat illness, results are hard to obtain. This is due to the quality of the compounding, and one cannot blame the shallowness or clumsiness of the formula; careful examination is needed. Further, the drugs in ancient formulas are mostly given in *zhu* and liang, and the water used is all stated in sheng; as the years recede further, errors appear in transmission and copying: some have small doses but much water, others much water and small doses; the weights are unequal and the vessel measures quite different. If one does not distinguish fine from coarse, how can one make clear what to take and what to discard? Now we have adjusted them to proper measure, set the doses at a mean, removed the inconsistencies of the old formulas, and fitted them to present-day use. In these formulas, *fen* means 2½ *qian*; liang means four *fen*; jin means sixteen liang. "Equal parts" (*deng fen*) does not mean the *fen* of weights, but that the weights of all the drugs are the same. When boiling decoctions, "one large *zhan* of water" is about 1 sheng; "one medium *zhan*" about 5 *ge*; "one small cup" about 3 *ge*. We have aimed at simplicity, hoping to avoid inconsistency, so that in compounding, decocting and mixing, when facing illness and rescuing emergencies, things will not be cumbersome and will be easy to understand. Herbs have roots, stems, branches, leaves, bark, wood, flowers and fruit; insects have hair, wings, skin, shell, head, legs, tail and bones; some must be burned, blast-fried or roasted, and raw or cooked is fixed; all must be done according to method. Those who follow the formula are blessed; those who go against it meet calamity. Sometimes the flesh is needed and the skin removed, sometimes the skin and the flesh removed; sometimes root and stem, sometimes flower and fruit are needed; select and refine according to the formula, peel and pare, make them extremely clean, and only then weigh out the doses without inconsistency. Drugs have mutual generation and mutual killing, their qi and strength are strong or weak, sovereign and minister employ each other; unless one is widely versed in the classics, one will not know their likes and dislikes. If a physician adjusts by his own notions without following the doses of the formula, so that the strong and weak of the drugs and minerals deceive each other, they enter the person's belly and cannot treat the illness; rather they attack each other, herbs and minerals oppose each other, causing confusion and derangement with a force worse than knives and swords. If the combination is right, even if the illness is not yet removed, the five zang organs can still be calmed and harmonized, and the illness is not aggravated.

In boiling decoctions, use well-flower water, made extremely clean. The amount of water must not vary. Always keep a gentle fire at a slight boil so that the flavor of the medicine comes out; in boiling and harmonizing one must apply care. Thus purging decoctions should be nearly raw, using little water and taking much; supplementing decoctions should be well cooked, using much water and taking little, wrung through new cloth. Decoctions are better taken slightly hot, for then they are easily digested and go down; if cold, they cause vomiting counterflow. "Divide into two or three doses" means that their force should reach continuously; also observe the person's strength and the severity of the illness, and advance, retreat, increase or decrease accordingly; one need not follow every word of the formula.

In pounding and sifting pill medicines, use heavy, close-woven silk to make them fine; mixed in honey they ripen easily. When sifting herbal medicines into powders, use light, fine silk; mixed and taken in wine they do not become muddy. Mineral medicines are also sifted through fine silk, then ground several hundred times, until color and texture are uniform.

When mineral medicines are used in decoctions or wine, pound them all finely and sift to the size of millet; they may also be evened with a kudzu sieve; wrap in new silk floss and decoct together in the decoction or wine. In compounding pills and powders, first cut finely, dry in the sun and then pound. Some are pounded separately, some together, all as the formula says. Moist medicines such as asparagus root and dried rehmannia are cut finely and sun-dried, pounded alone until thoroughly crushed, then taken out, torn finely and sun-dried; in overcast or rainy weather they may be dried over a gentle fire; when dry, let them rest and pound when cool.

Moist medicines all lose much weight when dried, so the dose should first be increased; weigh only once they are reduced to crumbs to have the correct amount. This is not necessary for decoctions and wine.

In steeping medicinal wines, chop everything finely, put it in raw silk, then into the wine, seal tightly, and according to the days suited to cold or heat, judge the strength and filter it out; there is no need to wait until the wine has been drunk to the end. The dregs may be sun-dried, lightly pounded and steeped again to drink, or made into a powder to take.

In compounding ointments, first soak the drugs in wine or vinegar until thoroughly permeated, without using much liquid, cover tightly so nothing escapes, from this morning to the next; some only overnight. Decoct over a gentle fire, putting on and taking off the fire three times to release the force of the heat and let the flavor of the medicines come out; put it on until it bubbles all over, then take it off; when the boiling has subsided for a good while, stop; it is better to be slightly underdone. Where there is Chinese chive bulb, the sign is that both ends are slightly scorched yellow; where there are angelica dahurica and aconite, also let them turn slightly yellow. Lard must never come into contact with water; that of the twelfth month is better still. Wring the ointment through new cloth. If it is an ointment to be taken internally, the dregs may also be boiled in wine and drunk; if it is an ointment to be rubbed on, the dregs should be applied to the affected place; this is to use up the full strength of the medicine. When realgar, cinnabar, musk, frankincense, red lead and the like are used in an ointment, grind each separately to powder and add only when the ointment is finished, stirring quickly with something until it sets, so that they do not sink and gather unevenly at the bottom. Where there is mercury or lead white, grind it extremely fine in the ointment.

In refining pills and powders of the divine immortals for prolonging life, first purify the room, burn incense, sweep and sprinkle, refrain from idle talk, and have a boy pound them, striving to make them fine and thoroughly worked; the number of poundings may reach ten thousand, and the more the better. Do not let women, children, those in mourning, women after childbirth, those with chronic illnesses, those lacking any of the six faculties, or the six domestic animals see them, or none will work. Small urgent decoctions are not subject to this rule.

## Notes

On the method of compounding: selecting genuine, good drugs, correct preparation and dosage, not leaving compounding to others; rationalizing the confused weights and water volumes of ancient formulas; the units (1 *fen* = 2½ *qian*, 1 liang = 4 *fen*, 1 jin = 16 liang; meaning of "equal parts"; large *zhan* ≈ 1 sheng, medium ≈ 5 *ge*, small cup ≈ 3 *ge*); choosing and preparing parts; the dangers of ignoring the seven relations and arbitrary changes; how to boil decoctions (well-flower water, gentle fire; less water for purgatives, more for tonics) and take them; sifting pills and powders; handling minerals; drying moist drugs; steeping medicinal wines; making ointments; and the ritual for refining life-prolonging elixirs.
- Largely based on the compounding discussions of the *Qianjin fang* and similar works.
