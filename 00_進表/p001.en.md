# 太平惠民和劑局方 進表 p001 昔神農嘗百草之味，以救万民之疾；周官設疾… (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Of old, Shennong tasted the flavours of the hundred herbs to save the myriad people from illness, and the Zhou offices established the post of physicians for illnesses to take charge of the people's diseases. These were recorded in books and became the model for ten thousand generations. Since our Song dynasty arose, sage rulers have handed on [the throne] one to another, all nurturing living beings with supreme benevolence and great virtue. They considered that epidemics come repeatedly and arise in every season, and that among the arts of relief nothing comes before books of formulas. Therefore from the Kaibao era onward they early commanded close officials to collate the materia medica; later they compiled the *Shenyi pujiu*, published the *Taiping shenghui*, revised the *Zhen'ai shuxue*, collated the *Qianjin* and *Waitai*, and made formularies such as the *Qingli shanjiu* and *Jianyao jizhong*, to benefit the realm. Some were cast in metal and carved in stone, some were cut on woodblocks and bound in volumes. Even the solicitude of Shennong and the government of the flourishing Zhou could not surpass this. Heaven bestowed [the throne] on Emperor Shenzong, who continued the line with sagely wisdom; his virtue of loving life was not shown only in formulas and treatises. He also established the Imperial Medical Bureau's Dispensary of Prepared Medicines in the capital; his compassion for the people's sufferings may be called diligent. His present Majesty, deeply benevolent by nature, filially continues the achievements of his predecessors. From the Chongning era he added seven bureaus, displaying the names "Harmonious Preparations" (hezi) and "Benefiting the People" (huimin), and had manufacture and sale each assigned to its office. He also established the Office for Purchasing Medicinal Materials to correct the abuses of counterfeit and inferior goods. Recently he decreed that all major prefectures set up pharmacies, in order to extend the beneficence of his ancestors; this may be called thorough. Yet since the bureaus were founded, their formulas have been taken partly from families who sell medicines and partly from men who presented them, without having been checked, and are not free of errors. Although they were once printed and issued, they could not avoid transmitting doubtful points and inheriting mistakes. Thus there are omitted ingredients and wrong weights; methods of preparation often do not follow the classics, what has been handed down is sometimes false, and the posted notices are especially full of errors, too many to list one by one. Recently I submitted these matters point by point to the court, and then received the command to select physicians versed in medicine and have them correct [the formulas]. We therefore requested the secret texts of the Imperial Library and gathered the separate records of famous worthies, searched public and private copies without omission, and investigated even matters lacking a source. Sometimes we rectified the root to correct the branches, sometimes traced the stream back to its source; we corrected the errors and resolved the confusion. What was lost we supplied, what was duplicated we deleted, and the book was completed within a year. As soon as the fair copy was finished, we respectfully present it to the court. Those who compound medicines will obtain complete efficacy, and those who take them will have not the slightest doubt. Issuing this completed book, the benefit will reach the whole realm. Thus the fine intention of benefiting the people in the Xining and Yuanfeng eras and the great plan of continuing that work in the Chongning and Daguan eras will be fully set forth in every part, great and small. It will bring this people to longevity and health and summon harmonious qi between heaven and earth, to be handed down without end for millions of years. Is this not splendid!

## Notes

The memorial presenting the book, attributed to Chen Shiwen and others when they revised the bureau's formulary in the Daguan era (1107–1110).
- It recounts the Song court's medical publications (collation of the materia medica, publication of the *Taiping shenghui fang*, collation of the *Qianjin fang* and *Waitai miyao*, etc.) and the founding of the state dispensaries and bureaus.
- It explains that the bureau's formulas contained omitted ingredients, wrong doses and faulty preparation methods, and were therefore collated against many copies by trained physicians.
- "Shenkao" refers to Emperor Shenzong and "His present Majesty" probably to Huizong; Xifeng = the Xining and Yuanfeng eras (Shenzong), Chongguan = the Chongning and Daguan eras (Huizong).
