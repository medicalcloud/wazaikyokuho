# 太平惠民和劑局方 指南總論 卷上 005 論三品薬畏悪相反 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

On the mutual fear, aversion and opposition of drugs of the three grades.

Investigating the natures of all things, all have separations and unions. When the tiger roars wind arises; when the dragon chants clouds rise; the lodestone draws the needle; amber picks up mustard seeds; lacquer meeting crab disperses; hemp meeting lacquer wells up; cinnamon meeting scallion softens; a tree meeting cinnamon withers; rock salt stacks eggs; otter bile divides a cup. Their qi responds to one another in many such ways, and the principle cannot be grasped by thought. As for drugs, they especially bring benefit and harm to one another. Since the former sages have clearly spoken of this, how can one not examine it and avoid [the harm]? People today in composing formulas are mostly negligent. If the old formulas already have [such combinations], these too should be revised and removed; if two kinds are equivalent, choose and remove one according to their weight. In the red powder for cold damage I always leave out veratrum, and in the Huanglian wan for stopping diarrhea I also remove the dried ginger, and they never fail to work. Why force things that hate each other to work together? The harm of mutual opposition is deeper than that of mutual aversion. Mutual aversion means that though the other dislikes me, I have no anger; as bovine bezoar is averse to dragon bone, yet dragon bone with bovine bezoar becomes even better, because there is something that restrains. Mutual opposition means that the two are enemies, and must not be combined. Painters today find that orpiment and lead white placed near each other darken and spoil each other: the white turns black with the yellow, and the yellow also changes with the white. This is evidence of mutual opposition. Because the principles of drugs are not understood, they do not work, and people mostly make light of this. Now, fearing that when treating according to formulas it will be hard to consult the *Bencao* at short notice, I have again copied these matters out here, so that they can be easily known and verified at a glance. The *Classic* sometimes says simply "zhuyu" or "mendong", without distinguishing mountain (cornus) from Wu (evodia), or tian (asparagus root) from mai (ophiopogon); each should be given its own entry. There are also confused errors: for example, clam shell and alligator shell have exactly the same fears and aversions; and the various *zhi* fungi are said to have Chinese yam as envoy, while Chinese yam in turn has purple *zhi* as envoy—this cannot be right, and as I do not know which is correct, I record both for now, to be verified more widely and corrected. Further, the *Shennong Classic* gives only one mutual assistant for each, but comparing with the *Yaodui*, some have two or three, which does no harm. Those said to "work together to treat the illness" are not prohibitions to avoid and are not listed again.

The 120 upper-grade drugs are sovereigns; they govern the nourishment of life and correspond to heaven; they are non-toxic, and much or long use does not harm a person; they are for those who wish to lighten the body, augment qi, not age and prolong life. The upper-grade drugs can also all dispel illness, but their force is very mild and rich and does not give sudden effect; yet taken regularly over years they surely bring great benefit: not only is the illness cured, but life is also extended. The way of heaven is to nurture with benevolence, hence "corresponding to heaven". The 120 kinds correspond to the months *yin*, *mao*, *chen* and *si*, following the time when all things grow and flourish.

The 120 middle-grade drugs are ministers; they govern the nourishment of the nature and correspond to humankind; some are non-toxic and some toxic, to be weighed as fitting; they are for those who wish to check illness and supplement deficiency and weakness. In the middle grade, the language of treating illness grows deeper and that of lightening the body somewhat thinner; for those who take them, the removal of ailments is quick but the prolongation of life slower. People harbor natures and emotions, hence "corresponding to humankind". The 120 kinds correspond to the months *wu*, *wei*, *shen* and *you*, following the time when all things ripen.

The 125 lower-grade drugs are assistants and envoys; they govern the treatment of illness and correspond to earth; they are mostly toxic and must not be taken for long; they are for those who wish to remove cold, heat and pathogenic qi, break accumulations and cure illness. The lower grade governs attack exclusively; their fierce, toxic qi upsets and damages the balance, so they must not be taken regularly; stop when the illness is cured. The body of earth gathers in and kills, hence "corresponding to earth". The 125 kinds correspond to the months *xu*, *hai*, *zi* and *chou*, following the time when all things wither, with the surplus of the intercalary days added. The three grades of the *Shennong Classic* total 365 kinds, following the 365 degrees, one degree corresponding to one day, making one year. The headings are given here to clarify the numbers of drugs in each grade; drugs added in the Tang to the *Bencao* and those added after use by famous physicians are not included.

## Notes

On fears, aversions and oppositions of drugs of the three grades: using examples of sympathetic responses among things (the lodestone and the needle, etc.), it urges that drug aversions and oppositions be examined and avoided; distinguishes mutual aversion (one restrains the other but they can coexist) from mutual opposition (enemies that must not be combined; the example of orpiment and lead white); notes confusions in the *Classic*; and describes the 120 upper-grade drugs (sovereigns, corresponding to heaven, nourishing life, non-toxic, for long use), 120 middle-grade (ministers, corresponding to humankind, nourishing the nature) and 125 lower-grade (assistants and envoys, corresponding to earth, treating illness, toxic, not for long use), 365 in all, following the days of the year.
- From the preface of Tao Hongjing's *Bencao jing jizhu* (the "I" who omits veratrum is Tao Hongjing).
